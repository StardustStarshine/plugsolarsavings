How Much Does Plug-in Solar Cost in the UK?

Understand plug-in solar kit prices, what is included, possible extra costs and how to compare the purchase price with potential household savings.

Last checked: 16 September 2026 · Great Britain rules can differ from Northern Ireland

There is no single universal UK price for plug-in solar. The amount you pay depends on the number and type of panels, inverter, mounting method, accessories, delivery and whether a battery or other services are included.

As a current market illustration, recent September 2026 checks of retailer listings found complete plug-in kits in Great Britain ranging from roughly £400 to around £1,000, with some offers outside that range. These are observed retail examples, not a guaranteed typical price or a recommendation. Prices, stock and included equipment can change quickly.

The most useful comparison is the total cost of a suitable, usable system, rather than the headline price alone.

What does plug-in solar cost?

The purchase price of a plug-in solar kit is usually made up of several components. Not every product includes all of them, so two kits with similar advertised prices may provide different amounts of equipment.

Solar panels

The panels generate DC electricity. The number of panels and their combined watt-peak rating can affect the price.

Inverter

The inverter converts the panels' electricity into AC electricity for the intended connection. Its model and specification matter.

Mounting

Ground, wall, balcony or other mounting equipment may be included, optional or sold separately.

Cables & connectors

Check whether the required cables, connectors, plugs and related accessories are part of the advertised package.

Monitoring

Some products include an app, gateway or other monitoring equipment; others may not.

Delivery and extras

Delivery, accessories, professional advice or electrical work may affect the final amount paid.

What are current plug-in solar kit prices like?

Retail prices are moving as the Great Britain plug-in solar market develops. Recent independent checks of named UK retailer listings in September 2026 showed examples from approximately £400 to £1,000 for complete kits, depending on the panel array, mounting and package contents.

For context, one September price comparison recorded complete kits from £428 to £989, while another market check identified offers beginning below £450. These figures should be treated as a snapshot of advertised prices on particular dates, not as a fixed UK price list.

Important: A price range is only useful when the products being compared are genuinely equivalent. A lower-priced kit may have fewer panels, different mounting, fewer accessories, delivery excluded or other items that another seller includes.

Before relying on a price, open the retailer's current product page and check the exact contents, delivery terms, warranty information and compliance documentation.

Advertised kit price vs total cost

The advertised kit price is the amount shown for the product or bundle. The total cost is what you may need to pay to obtain a suitable system and have it ready for its intended use.

For example, one advertised package might include panels, an inverter, mounting and cables. Another might show a lower headline price but require mounting equipment, monitoring hardware, delivery or other accessories to be purchased separately. That does not automatically make either product better or worse; it means the prices are not directly comparable until the contents are clear.

Before comparing prices, ask:

  • Is the price for a complete kit or only part of a system?
  • How many panels are included, and what is their combined capacity?
  • Which inverter model is included?
  • Is mounting included, and is it suitable for the intended location?
  • Are cables, connectors and the required plug included?
  • Is monitoring included?
  • Are delivery charges included?
  • Are any professional checks or electrical costs separate?

What affects plug-in solar prices?

Several factors can change the cost of plug-in solar in the UK:

  • Panel capacity and number: More panel capacity can increase the amount of equipment supplied and therefore the price.
  • Panel specification: Panel technology, dimensions, construction and manufacturer can affect cost.
  • Inverter specification: Different inverter models and included monitoring arrangements can have different prices.
  • Mounting method: Ground, wall, balcony and other mounting solutions may require different hardware.
  • Included accessories: Cables, connectors, brackets, monitoring devices and protective items may be included or optional.
  • Battery storage: Adding storage changes the system and budget considerably.
  • Brand and warranty: Warranty terms, support arrangements and the seller can affect the price.
  • Retailer and delivery: Different sellers may charge different prices or delivery fees, and promotions can expire.
  • Property-specific requirements: Permissions, mounting constraints or professional advice may add costs in some situations.

400W vs 800W plug-in solar systems

A smaller system may have a lower purchase price, but the relationship is not simply “double the panel capacity equals double the cost”. Panel count, inverter arrangement, mounting, included accessories and retailer pricing all affect the final figure.

When researching an 800W plug-in solar cost, remember that the number used in product descriptions may refer to different things. Panel capacity is generally expressed in Wp, while the inverter has its own AC output and apparent-power ratings. The current Great Britain route has defined requirements for qualifying products, so check the exact specification rather than comparing a single number.

A larger panel array may also be paired with an inverter whose AC output is lower than the combined panel rating. That is why the complete product specification matters more than the headline wattage.

How much does plug-in solar with a battery cost?

A battery can substantially change the overall budget because it adds storage equipment and may involve a different system design, monitoring arrangement or installation requirements.

The basic reason to consider storage is that solar generation and household electricity demand do not always happen at the same time. A battery may allow some generated electricity to be used later, but the financial value depends on the system, usage pattern, tariffs, battery behaviour and other factors.

Do not assume that a battery is included in a standard plug-in solar price. The current Great Britain plug-in solar product route described by the government is for qualifying plug-in solar systems without batteries. Explore the separate topic on our Solar Batteries page.

What about installation and electrical costs?

Some plug-in solar products are designed for a simpler connection route, but that does not mean every property, mounting arrangement or electrical situation is identical. The total cost may depend on the product instructions, the intended location, the condition of the socket and circuit, mounting requirements and whether professional advice or work is appropriate.

Do not assume that a product advertised as plug-and-play removes every responsibility or property-specific consideration. If you are unsure about the suitability of the electrical installation or mounting location, seek qualified professional advice rather than altering wiring or improvising a connection.

For safety and regulatory considerations, see UK solar rules and safety. Great Britain and Northern Ireland should not be treated as having identical arrangements; check the requirements that apply where you live.

What am I actually paying for?

Use this price-check checklist

Before buying or comparing a quote, record the answers to each of these questions:

  1. How many panels are included?
  2. What is the combined panel capacity in Wp?
  3. Which inverter model is supplied?
  4. What are the inverter's relevant AC output and apparent-power ratings?
  5. Is mounting included, and what type is it?
  6. Are cables, connectors and plugs included?
  7. Is monitoring included?
  8. Is a battery included, or is it a separate purchase?
  9. What warranties apply to each major component?
  10. Are delivery and collection charges included?
  11. Could professional advice, installation or electrical work be needed?
  12. Are any other components or permissions relevant to your property?

Cheapest is not always the same as lowest total cost

A lower advertised price can be attractive, but it may not be the lowest total cost if important components, delivery or suitable mounting are excluded. Conversely, a more expensive system is not automatically better value.

The sensible approach is to compare the complete specification, the equipment included, the warranty and support arrangements, the delivery terms and any additional costs. Keep product claims separate from independent estimates, and check current information before purchasing.

Cost vs potential savings

The purchase price should not be considered in isolation. The potential benefit depends on how much electricity the system generates and how much of that electricity your household can use.

  • Location and available sunlight
  • Panel orientation and mounting position
  • Shading
  • System size and panel capacity
  • Household electricity consumption
  • When electricity is used
  • Your electricity tariff
  • Whether a battery is present

For that reason, a lower-cost kit is not automatically the right choice for every home, and a higher-cost kit is not automatically a poor financial decision. The relevant question is how the complete cost relates to the electricity you may generate and use.

Illustrative example only

Imagine a system costs £600. If it generates electricity over a year, only some of that electricity may be used directly by the household, and the value of that usable electricity depends on the household's tariff and usage pattern. The relationship is therefore:

System cost → electricity generated → electricity used in the home → potential bill benefit

This is an explanation of the calculation process, not a forecast. It does not assume a particular annual generation figure, saving, payback period or product performance.
