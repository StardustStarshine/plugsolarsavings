Is Plug-in Solar Legal in the UK? Rules, Limits & Safety

Understand the rules, network requirements, 800W considerations and safety checks to explore before buying or connecting plug-in solar.

Last checked: 16 September 2026 · Great Britain rules can differ from Northern Ireland

Is plug-in solar legal in the UK? There is no single answer that applies to every product marketed as “plug-in solar”. The position depends on the equipment, its electrical characteristics, how it connects to the property, the applicable network requirements and the rules in the part of the UK where you live.

In Great Britain, the government announced a route for qualifying plug-in solar products from 27 August 2026. That does not mean every product labelled “800W”, “balcony solar” or “plug and play” automatically meets every requirement. You still need to check the complete system, its documentation and the circumstances of the installation.

What does “plug-in solar” actually mean?

“Plug-in solar” is a broad consumer term. It may describe a small solar photovoltaic system using panels, an inverter and connection equipment intended to supply electricity to a property through an appropriate connection arrangement.

The phrase does not identify one universal design. Panel DC capacity, inverter AC output, mounting method, protection, monitoring, batteries and the intended connection can all differ. A product’s ability to fit a plug into a socket is not, by itself, proof that the complete setup is suitable or compliant.

Great Britain and Northern Ireland: an important distinction

Great Britain means England, Scotland and Wales. The 2026 plug-in solar changes discussed in current government material concern Great Britain electrical-network arrangements. Northern Ireland has its own electricity network and regulatory framework, so readers there should not assume that a Great Britain product route, DNO process or technical statement automatically applies.

Before buying or connecting a system, check the requirements that apply to your location with the relevant network operator, supplier or suitably qualified professional.

What are G98 and G99?

G98 and G99 are Engineering Recommendations used in Great Britain in relation to connecting generation equipment to the electricity distribution network. They are not simply product marketing labels; they relate to technical requirements and the process for connecting generating equipment.

G98: broadly, simpler notification arrangements

G98 is commonly associated with certain small-scale, type-tested generating installations that meet the relevant conditions for a simplified connection and notification process. It should not be treated as a universal “below a particular wattage means G98” rule. The equipment, phase arrangement, total generation and applicable version of the requirements matter.

G99: broader or more involved connection requirements

G99 is associated with generating equipment and connection circumstances that fall outside the simpler G98 route or require the wider G99 process. Depending on the system, this can involve an application, technical information, assessment and approval before connection.

The correct route should be confirmed rather than guessed from a headline panel rating. The manufacturer, installer and relevant Distribution Network Operator (DNO) are useful sources for checking the applicable requirements.

What is a DNO and why does notification matter?

A Distribution Network Operator (DNO) operates the local electricity distribution network that delivers electricity to homes and businesses. A DNO is not the same as your electricity supplier.

Small generation connected to a property can affect the local network, which is why notification or approval requirements exist. Depending on the equipment and connection arrangement, the process may involve notifying the DNO after installation or applying for approval before connection. These are different concepts, and neither should be assumed away because a product is described as plug-in.

Practical rule: do not rely only on a retailer’s phrase such as “G98 compliant” or “no permission needed”. Check the actual product documentation, the relevant network requirements and the process specified for your installation.

What does 800W / 800VA mean?

People researching plug-in solar in Great Britain often encounter an 800W or 800VA figure. These figures need to be read in context.

  • Panel DC rating: Solar panels are commonly rated in watts peak (Wp). This describes their nominal DC generation capacity under stated test conditions.
  • Inverter AC output: The inverter converts DC electricity into AC electricity. Its output rating is a separate specification.
  • VA: Volt-amperes describe apparent power. VA and watts are related but are not automatically interchangeable in every technical context.

Current Great Britain government material and the associated product specification refer to an inverter maximum apparent power of 800VA and a maximum rated AC output of 800W for the relevant plug-in microgenerator route, alongside other requirements. This should not be read as “any 800W-labelled kit is automatically legal”. The complete product must satisfy the applicable conditions.

A kit can also have a combined panel DC rating higher than the inverter’s AC output. Therefore, do not use the panel Wp figure alone to determine the system’s AC output or compliance.

Electrical safety: plugging in is not the whole assessment

A safe system needs more than a suitable-looking plug. The equipment, protection, cables, connectors, socket or connection arrangement, mounting and wider electrical installation all need to be appropriate for the intended use.

Equipment and protection

Check the inverter documentation, protective functions, ratings, markings and compatibility of the supplied components.

Cables and connections

Use only the connection equipment specified by the manufacturer. Do not improvise connections or assume adapters are permitted.

Weather and damage

Outdoor equipment must be suitable for its environment. Inspect for damage, degradation, overheating or water ingress and follow the manufacturer’s instructions.

Electrical installation

If the socket, circuit, earthing, protection or condition of the installation is uncertain, seek qualified electrical advice.

Do not assume extension leads, multi-way adaptors, RCD adaptors or other accessories are acceptable. The relevant product specification and manufacturer instructions should be followed, and domestic wiring should not be altered as a DIY workaround.

Balconies, walls, gardens and roofs

The mounting location can affect safety, permissions and planning. Balcony systems, wall-mounted panels, garden frames and roof-mounted systems are not necessarily treated identically.

Consider structural loading, wind exposure, secure mounting, access, fire safety, cable routing, shared areas and whether the location is suitable for the equipment. Flats, leasehold properties and buildings with cladding or remediation restrictions may require additional checks.

Renters, leaseholders and shared buildings

If you rent or live in a leasehold or managed property, check your tenancy agreement, lease, building rules and insurance position. You may need permission from a landlord, freeholder, managing agent or residents’ management body, particularly where mounting, external appearance, shared space or structural fixings are involved.

Permission is not automatically required in every situation, but it should never be assumed that it is unnecessary.

Does plug-in solar need planning permission?

Planning requirements depend on the location and installation. Relevant factors can include whether equipment is mounted on a roof, wall or ground structure; whether the property is listed; whether it is in a conservation or other designated area; and whether the arrangement falls within applicable permitted development rules.

Do not assume that every plug-in or balcony installation is exempt. Check the planning framework for the nation and property concerned, and contact the local planning authority where necessary. Northern Ireland has a different planning framework from England, Scotland and Wales.

What is MCS, and does it make a system legal?

MCS stands for the Microgeneration Certification Scheme. It is a UK quality and certification scheme covering small-scale renewable-energy products, installers and installations in relevant contexts.

MCS certification, electrical/network compliance and planning permission are separate questions. MCS does not, by itself, establish that every product is legal, that a particular connection is permitted or that planning permission is unnecessary. Conversely, the absence of MCS should not automatically be described as proof that a system is illegal.

MCS can matter for consumer protection, installer standards and eligibility for certain schemes. For example, Ofgem’s Smart Export Guarantee guidance says eligible generators generally need to demonstrate suitable certification for the installation and installer, such as MCS or an equivalent scheme, alongside other requirements.

What happens if the system exports electricity?

If the solar system generates more electricity than the household is using at that moment, some electricity may flow to the grid. This is called export.

Export arrangements, metering and payment are separate from the basic question of whether equipment can be connected. A smart meter may be relevant to measuring export, but having one does not automatically make a system eligible for payments.

The Smart Export Guarantee (SEG) can provide payments from participating suppliers to eligible small-scale generators in Great Britain, subject to the scheme’s criteria. Eligibility can include requirements relating to certification, metering and the installation. Do not assume that every plug-in system automatically qualifies or that export payments are guaranteed.

What about batteries?

A battery can store some generated electricity for use later, which may be useful when generation and household demand occur at different times. However, adding a battery changes the equipment, cost, design and safety considerations.

Do not assume that a battery-integrated product falls within the same rules as a simple plug-in solar device. Check the product’s specific requirements and seek appropriate advice. You can read more on our Solar Batteries page.

What should I check before buying?

Use this checklist

  1. Identify the complete system, not just the panel wattage or marketing name.
  2. Check the panel DC capacity and the inverter AC output separately.
  3. Read the manufacturer’s technical documentation and safety instructions.
  4. Check what connection method is specified and whether the equipment is designed for that use.
  5. Confirm what G98, G99 or another network process applies, if relevant.
  6. Check whether DNO notification or an application is required.
  7. Check product markings, type-testing information and any required documentation.
  8. Assess the socket, circuit and wider electrical installation; obtain qualified advice where appropriate.
  9. Check mounting, weather exposure, structural and fire-safety considerations.
  10. If renting or leasehold, check permission, tenancy/lease and building-management requirements.
  11. Check whether planning permission or other property consents are relevant.
  12. Check insurance implications and whether export-payment eligibility is actually available.
  13. If you are in Northern Ireland, verify the rules that apply there rather than relying on Great Britain guidance.

Common plug-in solar myths

“If it plugs into a socket, it is automatically legal.”

False as a general rule. The connection method is only one part of the assessment. Equipment, protection, installation, network and property requirements still matter.

“800W means I do not need to check anything.”

Incorrect. 800W/800VA figures relate to particular inverter and connection requirements. They do not certify every product or property.

“MCS certification is what makes every system legal.”

Oversimplified. MCS, electrical safety, network rules, planning and export eligibility are related but separate matters.

“Because it is plug-in, anyone can install it without checking.”

Do not assume this. Follow the manufacturer’s instructions and obtain qualified advice where the electrical installation, mounting or connection circumstances require it.

“Solar panels always need planning permission.”

Not necessarily. Planning requirements depend on the installation and property, but exemptions should not be assumed without checking.

Do not rely on product marketing alone

Terms such as “800W”, “plug and play”, “balcony solar”, “no electrician required”, “MCS” and “G98 compliant” can be useful clues, but they are not substitutes for technical documentation and applicable requirements.

Look for the exact model, ratings, connection instructions, safety information, testing or registration evidence where relevant, and clear information about DNO notification or approval. If the information is vague, ask the seller for clarification before buying.

Where to verify the current position

For current information, start with authoritative sources rather than relying solely on retailer descriptions:

  • UK Government information on plug-in solar
  • Ofgem Smart Export Guarantee guidance
  • Ofgem information relating to G98 and G99 changes
  • MCS Foundation information

Requirements can change. Confirm the current position with the relevant DNO, manufacturer and suitably qualified professional before purchasing or connecting equipment.
