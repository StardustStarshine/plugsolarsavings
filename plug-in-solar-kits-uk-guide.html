Plug-in Solar Kits UK: What to Know Before You Buy

A practical guide to what a plug-in solar kit can include, what to check before buying and the important questions to ask about the product, inverter, panels, safety and your home.

Last checked: 16 September 2026 · Great Britain rules can differ from Northern Ireland

If you're looking for plug-in solar kits in the UK, it can be difficult to tell what is actually included in a kit and which specifications matter.

A plug-in solar setup can include solar panels, an inverter, cables, mounting equipment and other components. But the fact that a product is advertised as a "solar kit", "plug-and-play solar" or "balcony solar" does not by itself tell you whether it is suitable for your home or compliant with the current requirements.

In Great Britain, the rules changed on 27 August 2026 to allow qualifying plug-in solar products to be connected directly to a standard household socket. That route applies only where the product meets the defined requirements, so checking the actual product matters.

What is a plug-in solar kit?

A plug-in solar kit is a small-scale solar setup designed to generate electricity from solar panels for use in a home or other suitable property. A typical system can contain several connected components, rather than simply being a solar panel that you plug into a socket.

The exact contents vary between products. Before comparing prices, check exactly what the seller is supplying and whether the complete product is designed for the use being advertised.

Solar panels

The panels convert sunlight into DC electricity. Their rated power is normally expressed in watts peak (Wp).

Inverter

The inverter converts the panels' DC electricity into AC electricity suitable for the intended connection and use.

Mounting equipment

Depending on the kit, this may include brackets, a frame or other equipment used to position the panels.

Cables & connectors

The kit may include the cables and connectors needed to connect its components. Check what is actually supplied rather than assuming.

Don't compare kits by the panel wattage alone

One of the easiest mistakes when comparing plug-in solar kits is to look only at the number printed on the solar panels.

Solar panels are commonly described by their DC power rating in watts peak (Wp). The inverter has its own AC output and apparent-power rating. These are different measurements and they should not be treated as interchangeable.

For the current Great Britain plug-in solar route, the government specification sets an inverter maximum apparent power of 800 VA and a maximum current to the installation of 3.5 A. The product specification also allows the combined PV module rating to be higher than the inverter's AC output, subject to its own requirements.

In simple terms: a kit can have panels with a higher DC Wp rating than the inverter's AC output. So seeing a higher combined panel rating does not automatically mean the inverter is an equivalent AC-output device.

Always check the inverter's declared output and apparent power, as well as the panel specifications, rather than comparing one wattage figure in isolation.

What does 800W mean?

You will see "800W" frequently when researching plug-in solar in Great Britain. It is important to understand what the number refers to.

Under the current product specification, the inverter's declared maximum apparent power must not exceed 800 VA, while the regulations refer to a maximum rated AC output of 800 watts for the relevant plug-in microgenerator route. The specification also sets a 3.5 A maximum current to the installation.

This does not mean an 800W kit will produce 800W all day or generate the same amount of electricity every day. Solar generation changes with sunlight, location, orientation, shading, temperature and other conditions.

What should you check before buying?

The most important question is not simply "How many watts is this kit?" It is whether the complete product is appropriate for the intended use and meets the relevant requirements.

1. Check the inverter rating

For the current Great Britain plug-in solar specification, check the declared maximum apparent power, AC output and current. A seller should be able to give you clear technical information rather than relying only on marketing language.

2. Check the product markings

The current specification sets requirements for product markings, including manufacturer information, model identification, ratings, protective information, the one-device-per-circuit statement and a statement about mandatory DNO notification.

3. Check the ENA Type Test Register

For a compliant plug-in solar device, the manufacturer must have completed the required type testing and registered the device on the ENA Type Test Register before placing it on the market. A product listing alone is not proof of compliance.

4. Check what is actually included

Look at the full kit contents. Check the panel count and ratings, inverter model, cables, connectors, mounting equipment and any labels or documentation that should accompany the product.

5. Check the instructions before installing anything

The current product specification includes requirements and warnings covering sockets, circuits, extension cables, multi-way adaptors, RCD adaptors, mounting, cladding, balconies and other installation conditions. Read the manufacturer's instructions before buying or installing the kit.

Be careful with extension leads and adaptors

A plug-in solar kit is not simply an ordinary appliance that can be connected wherever convenient.

The current product specification says the device must not be connected using an extension cable, multi-way adaptor or RCD adaptor. It also requires warnings about damaged, degraded or non-compliant socket outlets.

If you are unsure which sockets are on the same household circuit, or whether the existing electrical installation is suitable, seek qualified electrical advice.

What about the panels?

Panels are another area where kit descriptions can become confusing. A higher panel Wp rating does not automatically mean the system will deliver that amount of AC power to the home.

When comparing panels, look at the manufacturer's specifications and consider how the panels will actually be positioned. Orientation, shading and available sunlight can have a significant effect on generation.

The Plug Solar Savings calculator takes these kinds of factors into account when producing its illustrative estimate. Its current model uses regional yield assumptions and adjusts the result for orientation and shade.

What about batteries?

A battery can be useful in a wider solar system because it can allow electricity generated during one period to be used later. However, batteries are a separate consideration from the current plug-in solar product route.

The current plug-in solar product specification specifically excludes plug-in battery systems and plug-in solar devices integrated with battery systems.

That does not mean batteries cannot be part of a wider home energy setup. It means you should not assume that a product containing a battery falls within the same compliant plug-in solar route.

Check your property before you buy

Even when a product is compliant, that does not automatically mean it is suitable for every property.

The manufacturer's documentation places responsibility on the user to obtain any permission needed from a landlord, freeholder, managing agent or other relevant authority, and to consider planning, insurance and property-specific requirements where applicable.

Flats, balconies and cladding need extra care

The current specification contains specific restrictions around certain cladding systems, timber balconies and buildings subject to external wall remediation. These are not issues to assume away just because a kit is advertised as "balcony solar".

If you rent, live in a flat or have a leasehold property, check the relevant permissions and property requirements before buying.

What about DNO notification?

DNO means Distribution Network Operator — the electricity network company responsible for the local distribution network.

The current plug-in solar specification requires the product to carry a statement that notification to the DNO about connection and disconnection is mandatory, together with information on how to do it.

So do not buy a kit because a seller says "no notification is needed" without checking the current requirements and the product's compliance information.

Great Britain and Northern Ireland are not the same

This is particularly important when reading online buying guides. The current plug-in solar route created by the 2026 regulatory changes is tied to Great Britain electrical-network arrangements. The Electricity Safety, Quality and Continuity Regulations amendment in SI 2026/848 extends to England, Wales and Scotland, while the plugs-and-sockets amendment has a different territorial extent.

If you are in Northern Ireland, do not assume that a guide written about Great Britain automatically applies to your installation. Check the rules that apply where you live before buying or connecting a system.

A simple plug-in solar kit checklist

Before buying, work through this list:

  1. Check the exact inverter model and declared AC/apparent-power rating.
  2. Check that the product documentation identifies the applicable current specification.
  3. Check the product markings and required plug markings.
  4. Check the manufacturer's ENA Type Test Register registration.
  5. Check exactly which panels, cables, connectors and mounting equipment are included.
  6. Read the installation and safety instructions before connecting anything.
  7. Do not assume extension leads, multi-way adaptors or RCD adaptors are permitted.
  8. Check the socket, circuit and wider electrical installation.
  9. Check landlord, freeholder, managing-agent, planning and insurance requirements where relevant.
  10. Check whether your planned mounting location has any fire-safety or cladding restrictions.
  11. If you are in Northern Ireland, check the rules that apply there rather than relying on Great Britain guidance.
