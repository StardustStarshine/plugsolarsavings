UK Solar Rules & Safety

What you need to know before buying or installing plug-in solar, batteries or other home solar systems.

Reviewed September 2026 · General educational information

Solar rules don't have to be confusing.

Terms such as G98, G99, DNO, MCS and SEG can make solar sound more complicated than it needs to be. This page explains the important points in plain English, with a focus on what an ordinary UK homeowner should check before buying or using solar equipment.

The rules below reflect the September 2026 position. They are general information, not a substitute for an assessment of your particular electrical installation, property or network connection.

First: is plug-in solar legal?

Yes — compliant plug-in solar systems can now be bought and used in Great Britain from 27 August 2026.

The important word is compliant. The new framework is for plug-in solar products that meet the applicable product, electrical and network requirements. A product being advertised online as “plug and play” does not automatically make it compliant.

The current framework is specifically for plug-in solar without batteries. Northern Ireland has separate arrangements.

UK rules for plug-in solar

What changed in 2026, and what the change actually means for homeowners.

The 2026 change

From 27 August 2026, compliant plug-in solar systems can be supplied and used in Great Britain under the new regulatory and product framework. The government introduced a specific route for plug-in solar microgenerators connected through a standard UK socket.

That does not mean every product sold as “plug and play solar” is automatically suitable. The product needs to meet the applicable specification, and the network connection and electrical installation requirements still matter.

The framework is also narrower than the phrase “plug-in solar” can suggest. It covers qualifying solar PV devices without batteries. Plug-in batteries and solar products integrated with batteries are outside the plug-in solar product specification.

The G98 information on this page mainly concerns Great Britain. Northern Ireland has separate electricity-network arrangements and residents should check the relevant local requirements before buying or connecting a system.

What does the 800W / 800VA rule mean?

The number that matters is the inverter's AC output — not simply the total wattage printed on the panels.

Panel watts and inverter output are different things.

Solar panels are commonly described by their DC power rating in watts peak (Wp). The inverter converts that DC electricity into AC electricity for the home.

Under the current plug-in solar specification, the inverter's declared maximum apparent power must not exceed 800VA. The inverter is also subject to a current limit and other technical requirements.

That means you should not look at a product's panel Wp figure on its own and assume it breaks the 800VA limit. A system can have a combined panel DC rating above 800Wp while its inverter's AC output remains within the applicable limit.

Simple rule of thumb

Wp = panel DC rating.
VA = inverter AC apparent-power rating.

For the qualifying plug-in route, check the exact inverter rating and the product's compliance information rather than relying on a headline such as “800W solar”.

What is a DNO and do you need to notify them?

The company that supplies your electricity and the company that operates the local network are not necessarily the same.

DNO means Distribution Network Operator.

Your electricity supplier is the company you pay for electricity. Your DNO operates the local electricity distribution network — the infrastructure that delivers electricity to your property.

When you add generation, the network operator needs the relevant information about what is connected. A plug does not remove that network requirement.

For qualifying G98 plug-in generation in Great Britain, the current notification process applies within 28 days of commissioning. Check the instructions supplied with the product and the current requirements for your DNO.

Why does notification matter?

Your home is normally a consumer of electricity. Solar also makes your property a source of generation. The DNO needs to understand what generation is connected to its network so that it can manage network safety, supply quality and the equipment connected nearby.

Electrical safety: what should you check?

Plug-in is a connection method, not a guarantee that every socket, circuit or home is suitable.

1

Check the electrical installation

Older or unknown installations deserve particular attention. If you are unsure about the condition of the wiring or protective devices, get a competent electrician to assess it.

2

Understand RCD protection

Electrical Safety First recommends checking that the RCD protection is suitable for plug-in solar, with particular concern about older Type AC RCDs and reverse current flow.

3

Use the supplied connection method

Follow the manufacturer's instructions and use the supplied plug, lead, mounting system and accessories as specified. Do not improvise electrical connections.

4

Never use extension leads

Do not connect plug-in solar through extension leads, cable reels, multiway adaptors or travel adaptors. These can introduce additional electrical and overheating risks.

5

Buy from a reputable source

Check the exact product and its compliance information. Be particularly cautious with unbranded products and unknown third-party marketplace sellers.

6

Don't add a second system

The current Great Britain arrangement is one qualifying plug-in solar system per household. A second socket does not create a second allowance.

Important safety point: Electrical Safety First currently recommends that households have at least a Type A bidirectional RCD protecting circuits intended for plug-in solar and says homes with Type AC RCDs should not use plug-in solar without first upgrading to the recommended protection. This is safety guidance from Electrical Safety First, not a replacement for an assessment of your individual installation.

Do you need an MCS-certified installer?

MCS and electrical legality are related to different questions.

Being allowed to use solar is not the same as being eligible for an export scheme.

MCS — the Microgeneration Certification Scheme — is a certification scheme covering small-scale renewable technologies and installations. It is not simply a blanket legal requirement for every solar installation.

Where export payments are concerned, however, certification can matter. The Smart Export Guarantee has its own eligibility requirements, and eligible generators may need to demonstrate that the installation is suitably certified. Always check the current requirements of the SEG supplier you are applying to.

So keep two questions separate: “Can I legally use this system?” and “Does this system qualify for this payment scheme?” They are not the same question.

What happens if you export electricity?

Using solar at home and being paid for exports are two different things.

1

Home use comes first

Solar electricity generated while your home is using electricity can reduce the amount of electricity you need to take from the grid.

2

Surplus can flow to the grid

If generation exceeds your home's instantaneous demand, surplus electricity may flow back through the connection to the grid.

3

Export is not automatically paid

Sending electricity to the grid does not by itself mean you receive a payment for it.

4

SEG is separate

The Smart Export Guarantee is a separate scheme under which eligible generators can receive payments from participating suppliers.

5

Eligibility applies

SEG eligibility requirements include matters such as the technology, capacity, certification and export metering arrangements.

6

Check the supplier

If you want export payments, apply to a SEG licensee and check its current tariff and application requirements.

What about solar batteries?

Do not assume the plug-in solar rules automatically apply to storage.

Batteries are a separate consideration.

The current plug-in solar product framework specifically covers plug-in solar systems without batteries. It does not extend to plug-in battery systems or plug-in solar PV devices integrated with batteries.

Batteries can involve different product, electrical and network requirements. A battery that happens to have a plug is therefore not automatically covered by the 800VA plug-in solar route.

For permanently connected batteries, larger solar-plus-storage systems, consumer-unit work or anything outside the standard plug-in solar configuration, use an appropriately qualified installer and check the relevant connection and product requirements.

Renting, flats, balconies and property permissions

Electrical permission and property permission are separate questions.

A

Renters

Check your tenancy agreement and ask your landlord or housing provider before installing equipment. Being electrically permitted does not automatically give you permission under the tenancy.

B

Flats & leasehold

Check your lease and speak to the freeholder, managing agent or property management company where required. Building rules can restrict equipment on balconies, external walls or communal areas.

C

Balconies

Consider the mounting, structure, weight, wind exposure, secure fixing, falling equipment, cables and access or escape routes. Balcony solar is not automatically permitted just because plug-in solar is permitted.

D

Gardens & outdoor spaces

Follow the manufacturer's mounting instructions and make sure sockets, cables, fixings and the physical location are suitable for outdoor conditions.

E

Listed or protected properties

Planning and property considerations can differ for listed buildings, conservation areas and other protected locations. Check the relevant local requirements before installation.

F

Physical safety

Equipment should not create a falling-object hazard, obstruct access or escape routes, or interfere with building safety arrangements. Use the intended mounting system.

Planning is separate from electrical rules. Solar equipment can involve planning, building, lease, landlord, freeholder, insurance or property-management considerations even where the electrical connection itself is permitted.

What should you check before buying?

A simple eight-point checklist for an ordinary homeowner.

  1. What exactly am I buying? Identify the complete product, inverter, panels, plug and accessories rather than relying on a headline wattage.
  2. Is the exact product compliant? Check the manufacturer's compliance information and the applicable plug-in solar specification.
  3. What is the inverter output? Look for the inverter's AC apparent-power rating and do not confuse VA with panel Wp.
  4. Is my electrical installation suitable? If the installation, socket, circuit or RCD protection is unknown, get it assessed before use.
  5. Do I need DNO notification? For qualifying G98 generation, the applicable DNO notification process still applies.
  6. Do I have property permission? Check landlord, freeholder, managing agent, lease, tenancy and any relevant local requirements.
  7. Do I want export payments? If so, investigate SEG eligibility, certification and export-meter requirements separately.
  8. Am I considering a battery? If yes, do not assume the plug-in solar framework covers it. Check the separate requirements.

When should you speak to an electrician or installer?

This page is not a DIY electrical installation guide.

Professional advice is particularly sensible if you have:

  • an older or unknown electrical installation;
  • uncertainty about RCDs or other protective devices;
  • a damaged socket, plug, cable or wiring;
  • an unusual property, communal electrical system or complex building arrangement;
  • hardwired solar or other fixed electrical work;
  • consumer-unit or distribution-board work;
  • a larger solar generation system;
  • a battery or solar-plus-storage system;
  • structural mounting work that needs assessment; or
  • a system outside the standard qualifying plug-in configuration.

A qualified electrician or appropriately qualified installer can assess the parts of the project that cannot safely be answered from a general web page.

UK solar rules & safety FAQs

Plain-English answers to the questions homeowners commonly ask.

Is plug-in solar legal in the UK?

Compliant plug-in solar systems can now be bought and used in Great Britain from 27 August 2026, provided the product and connection meet the applicable requirements.

Northern Ireland has separate arrangements, so the Great Britain G98 route described here should not simply be assumed to apply there.

What is the 800W rule?

The relevant plug-in solar framework uses a maximum inverter apparent power of 800VA. This is an AC inverter output limit.

It should not be confused with the total DC panel rating in Wp. A system can have panels with a combined DC Wp rating above 800Wp while the inverter remains within the applicable 800VA limit.

Do I need to tell my DNO?

Yes, for qualifying G98 generation the applicable DNO notification process still applies. The current G98 timing is within 28 days of commissioning.

Your DNO is the local Distribution Network Operator, not necessarily the company you buy electricity from.

What is G98 vs G99?

G98 is the simplified route for qualifying small, fully type-tested generation. G99 is the more involved connection process for systems outside the applicable G98 route.

The exact route depends on the equipment and network requirements, not just a product's marketing description.

Do I need MCS?

MCS is not simply a blanket legal requirement for every solar installation. However, certification can be relevant to eligibility for schemes such as the Smart Export Guarantee.

If export payments matter to you, check the current SEG eligibility requirements rather than assuming that having solar automatically qualifies you.

Can I use plug-in solar with a battery?

The current plug-in solar product framework is specifically for plug-in solar without batteries. Plug-in batteries and plug-in solar products integrated with batteries are outside that framework.

Batteries can have different product, electrical and network requirements, so do not treat a battery as automatically covered by the 800VA plug-in solar route.

Can renters use plug-in solar?

Electrical legality does not automatically give a renter permission to install equipment. Check your tenancy agreement and obtain any permission required from your landlord or housing provider.

Can I put solar on a balcony?

Some systems are designed for balcony use, but plug-in solar being permitted does not make every balcony installation automatically suitable.

Consider mounting, structural suitability, weight, wind exposure, secure fixing, falling equipment, cables, access and escape routes, and any landlord, freeholder or building permissions.

Can I use an extension lead?

No. Plug-in solar should be connected using the manufacturer's specified connection method. Do not use extension leads, cable reels, multiway adaptors or travel adaptors.

Can I install two 800W systems?

The current Great Britain arrangement limits plug-in solar to one qualifying plug-in solar system per household. Do not treat different sockets or circuits as permission to install multiple systems.

Will I automatically get paid for exported electricity?

No. Surplus electricity may flow to the grid, but payment is separate. The Smart Export Guarantee is available where the applicable eligibility requirements are met and you apply to a participating SEG supplier.

Does the GB route apply to Northern Ireland?

The G98 information on this page mainly relates to Great Britain. Northern Ireland has separate electricity-network arrangements, so Northern Ireland residents should check the relevant local requirements before buying or connecting a system.
